What I believe

Four rules I work by.

  1. One honest conversation beats ten polished emails.
  2. Proposals should be walked through, not sent. The document is the leave-behind. The conversation is the pitch.
  3. Clarity wins. You don't need the flashiest idea. You need to know what you want, and you can usually tell within the first 30 minutes of a call.
  4. If it's meant for human eyes, write it yourself. Use AI for the work. Don't use it for the thinking.

          N°00718 Oct 2026

          Show the guardrails first

          What I took from N°005, Alexandr Wang on how Meta made its agent safe to say yes to, and how it changes the way I sell design work.

          • Show the guardrails before the portfolio. Enterprise buyers aren't asking whether we're good. They're asking what happens when something goes wrong. Answer that first.
          • Sell to people who don't want the tech. Most clients don't want to learn UX. They want fewer drop-offs and a product their users love. Talk about the outcome, not the craft.
          • Name their part up front. Same as the $10,000 line. Feedback on time, one decision-maker, access to real users. Write it into the kickoff, like scope.
          • Get paid when it works. Not every deal can be outcome-based, but tying part of it to a result is how you earn the next one.

          The takeaway isn't really about AI. People don't buy the smartest option. They buy the one they'd trust with the keys.

          N°00618 Oct 2026

          Show the cut marks

          Notes from Joe Rogan Experience #2558 with Tyler Engle, a musician and researcher who hosts the BassForge channel. Two and a half hours on old legends that might be true: a great flood, giants, and stone cut too cleanly for the tools of its time.

          I can't judge most of it, and most archaeologists reject the big claims. I listened for how they argue, not to pick a side.

          The flood that covered the world

          They start with the flood stories, from the Sumerian Ziusudra to Noah. Their take: the flood may have been local, somewhere around the Mediterranean or Anatolia, and the people who lived through it wrote down the only world they knew. 05:13 ↗

          If the water covered everything you could see, it covered the whole world.

          The evidence does the work

          Engle is at his best when he points at something real. The Kailasa Temple in India, carved down into a single rock. Cut marks in hard stone, and what he says is inside them. A clip of Liam Richards chanting in a rock-cut hall where the sound hangs for 15 to 20 seconds. 14:12 ↗

          You don't need to buy his theory to remember those.

          Where it loses me

          Then the claims run past the evidence: mummies as bookmarks for consciousness, pyramids as chemical plants powered by currents in the ground. 55:04 ↗

          Each step away from the stone costs a little trust. By the end, the strongest points sound weaker because of what came after them.They also spend a stretch on academics dismissing new ideas. Sometimes fair. But complaining about the gatekeepers rarely gets you through the gate.

          People believe what they can see. Everything past that, you're asking them to trust you.

          N°00517 Oct 2026

          Trust is the product

          Notes from Cleo Abram's Huge Conversations with Alexandr Wang, Meta's chief AI officer. The subject is Muse, Meta's personal agent. It hit 5 million downloads in 22 days and took the No. 1 free spot on the App Store, a place ChatGPT usually holds.

          I went in expecting a capability pitch. Most of the hour was about something else: why anyone should let an agent near their inbox and their card.

          From chatting to doing

          A chatbot answers. An agent does: chasing healthcare paperwork, cancelling a subscription you forgot about, filling in the form you've been putting off.

          Wang's point was that the industry has spent its energy making agents better at coding. "We really felt this was a big gap," he said. The people Muse is built for "don't know how to code. They probably never even want to code."That's most of the world, and most of the buyers I talk to. They don't want the tool. They want the thing the tool gets done.

          How they make it safe to say yes

          This is the part I'd steal. They didn't ask for trust. They built it into the product, one layer at a time.

          Sandbox
          Each agent runs in its own secure virtual machine. What you share stays inside it.
          Sentinel
          A second agent checks anything going out, like a form or a phone call, and blocks card and ID numbers.
          Permission
          A new website, or sending your data out, needs your yes first.
          Next
          Confidential virtual machines with stronger encryption, closer to WhatsApp-level privacy. Slower, and they say so.
          Ads
          Your chats and agent data stay out of Meta's ad system.

          Each layer answers an objection before the user raises it. That's exactly how a good proposal should read.

          Who pays for it

          Not ads, at least for now. "I wouldn't say we're spending that much time thinking about ads necessarily right now." The idea he floated is a small fee when Muse buys something for you or saves you money.

          I like that model. Muse only gets paid when it delivers, the way a good partner should.

          You still have to do your part

          People have been asking Muse to make them $10,000. Wang finds it funny, and his answer is the line I kept:

          "You're going to have to do your part if you want to make $10,000."

          He also said an agent's memory "is not like that of a human. Sometimes it can feel like it's like that, but it is really different." Useful to remember when it feels like it knows you.

          The uncomfortable bit

          Cleo pushed on safety, and he didn't dodge. He summed up the race logic as "I am responsible, those people are not, so I cannot slow down," and admitted nobody yet knows how to oversee systems much smarter than us. He also said AI leaders are all very smart, but personal grudges are shaping how this gets built, and that he's changed his mind on China: the "monster" framing goes too far.

          Outside the episode, Amazon has already blocked Muse from shopping on its site, citing data security.The title calls it the biggest launch since ChatGPT. Downloads aren't retention. I'll check back in three months. Trust isn't just the user's call. Every platform the agent touches gets a vote.

          Founders buy speed, enterprises buy safety

          Same proposal, two buyers. Each reads the first paragraph looking for one thing.

          Founder
          "How fast can I ship?"
          Enterprise
          "What happens if this goes wrong, and who gets blamed?"

          Pitch speed to the enterprise and you sound reckless. Pitch governance to the founder and you sound slow.Scope, price and timeline barely change between the two. Only the framing does.

          So I write the first paragraph twice. Everything below it stays the same.

          N°00328 Sep 2026

          Scope creep starts at kickoff

          Most scope fights trace back to week one. Someone assumed something, and nobody said it out loud.

          So I end every kickoff with one question.

          "What would make you unhappy in month two, even if we hit every deliverable?"Ask the most senior person in the room, and ask them last. The answer changes when the boss is listening.

          The answers are the real scope. Then three steps.

          • Write them down.
          • Put them in the recap.
          • Get a yes.

          N°00214 Sep 2026

          The recap email is the contract people read

          Nobody rereads the statement of work. Everyone rereads the email you sent after the call.

          So that email carries the weight. Keep it short, cut the adjectives, and cover four things.

          • Decisions. What we agreed.
          • Owners. One name per decision.
          • Dates. When each thing happens.
          • Open questions. What's still unresolved, and who answers it.

          If the stakeholder can't forward it to their boss without editing, it isn't finished.Second test. Could the client's CFO read it cold and know what happens next?

            Why they get better

            Every agent runs the same loop. Tap a step, or watch it go round.

            I send a message on Telegram: a receipt, a reminder, a call to prep for.

            It uses its tools to get it done: web search, a browser, vision, a terminal, and subagents that each work in their own sandbox.

            Once it has solved the problem, it writes down how as a reusable skill.

            Next time something similar comes up, it starts from that skill. That's why Plutus now files a receipt from a single photo.

            Hermes is one agent that learns. To run a whole team of agents, Hermes included, see Paperclip.
